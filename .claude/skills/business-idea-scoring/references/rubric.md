# Anchored rubric

Score each criterion 0-5. Anchors are given for 5, 3, and 1; use 4 and 2 for the obvious
in-between cases and 0 for "structurally impossible", which is a real and useful score.

The anchors exist because unanchored scoring drifts upward — every criterion feels like a
4 when you're excited. Match the idea to the *nearest anchor sentence*, not to a vibe, and
if it sits between two, take the lower one. Founders are not harmed by a score that is one
point too harsh; they are harmed by one that is three points too kind.

## 1. Distribution (w15)

*Do you already have a channel to the first 50 customers?*

Count named, reachable people who are themselves the buyer. Not "the industry", not
"people who know people". If reaching the buyer requires an introduction, that is a hop,
and each hop costs about a point.

- **5** — The founder can name 50+ people who are the buyer, could message them this week, and would expect replies because of who she is. Or she owns a channel that already reaches that many of them repeatedly.
- **4** — 20-50 named warm buyers, plus a credible repeatable route to the rest (a professional community she's part of, a publication that would run her, a partner with the list).
- **3** — 10-20 warm buyers, and the remainder needs cold outreach into a segment where her credentials genuinely open doors (analysts, PMs, architects). Also the ceiling for a route that depends on a handful of family or spousal introductions, however senior.
- **2** — The network is adjacent rather than the buyer: she knows people near the buyer and needs them to make introductions.
- **1** — Cold outreach into a segment where she has no credential advantage, or a channel she would have to build from zero (audience, SEO, paid) before anyone hears about it.
- **0** — The buyer is structurally unreachable for a solo founder: enterprise procurement, mass consumer requiring paid acquisition, or a gatekept channel she cannot enter.

Common error: scoring the *market's* accessibility rather than *her* access to it. A large
addressable market with no warm route is a 1, not a 4.

## 2. Demand intensity (w15)

*Will someone pay for a crude v1?*

Score the evidence, not the reasoning. A compelling argument that the pain exists is worth
2 at most; someone's money is worth 5. Ideas fail here more often than anywhere else, and
this is the criterion most improved by two weeks of work.

- **5** — Someone has asked to be invoiced, pre-paid, or is visibly paying for a worse substitute today. The pain is expensive, recurring, and already has a budget line.
- **4** — Multiple specific people have said "I would pay £X for that" about a defined thing at a defined price, unprompted by leading questions.
- **3** — Strong qualitative interest from real buyers who have described the pain in their own words, but no money and no price discussed.
- **2** — The pain is inferred from the founder's own experience of the job, and it's a good inference, but no external buyer has confirmed it. **This is the cap when there is no evidence.**
- **1** — "Nice idea, you should build that" reactions, or interest from people who aren't the buyer.
- **0** — The pain is theoretical, or it's a vitamin: real but no one reorganises their week or their budget over it.

Guard: the founder's own certainty is not evidence, and neither is a friendly network's
enthusiasm. Warm networks are systematically encouraging — that is what makes them warm.

## 3. Founder-market fit (w12)

*Does this founder have an unfair advantage here — credibility, speed, taste, or access?*

Read `founder-profile.md` first. Fit means she does this specific thing better, faster, or
more credibly than a capable stranger, in a way the buyer can perceive.

- **5** — The idea requires exactly what she has and would be hard for most people to start: institutional-finance judgement, or design and built-environment fluency, or AI-workflow change management in professional services — ideally two of the three. She has already done a version of the work, and the buyer will recognise her as one of them.
- **4** — Squarely inside one of her domains, with clear credibility to the buyer, but a competent outsider could learn it in a year.
- **3** — Adjacent to her experience: transferable skills (research speed, writing, modelling, design judgement) but no domain credibility with this specific buyer.
- **2** — She'd be learning the domain and the customer at the same time as building.
- **1** — The idea requires skills she doesn't have and can't quickly hire — production engineering, a licensed profession, deep sales.
- **0** — It requires a credential, licence, or track record she cannot obtain in the relevant window.

Do not inflate this because the idea is *interesting* to her. Interest is not fit. And note
that high founder-market fit paired with low distribution is the classic expert's trap: an
excellent product nobody hears about.

## 4. Revenue structure (w8)

*Recurring beats one-off. What does the second year look like without new sales?*

- **5** — Contracted recurring revenue: annual subscription or retainer, auto-renewing, embedded enough that renewal is the default.
- **4** — Monthly subscription with ordinary churn, or a retainer that renews by habit.
- **3** — Repeat purchase without a contract: customers reliably come back, but each return is a fresh decision.
- **2** — Project work with the same clients over time — real repeat business, but every pound requires a new sale.
- **1** — One-off sales with no natural repeat.
- **0** — One-off, long sales cycle, and each sale requires a fresh chase from cold. The treadmill.

## 5. Defensibility in 3 years (w8)

*If it works, what stops the fifth entrant, or an incumbent adding a feature?*

Score only what compounds. Effort, taste, and expertise make v1 good; they don't hold
position.

- **5** — Something accumulates with use: proprietary data that improves the product, network effects, deep workflow embedding with real switching cost, or an owned distribution asset that grows on its own.
- **4** — Strong brand or trust in a niche, plus a body of work or dataset that would take a competitor years to reproduce.
- **3** — Relationships and reputation, plus accumulated content or process. Copyable, but not cheaply or quickly.
- **2** — Execution quality and taste only. A well-funded competitor could match it in months.
- **1** — Rebuildable in a weekend by anyone with the same idea, or a thin layer over someone else's platform.
- **0** — Already commoditised, or one incumbent product update away from being redundant.

For anything built on a foundation model API, ask what the founder owns when the model
provider ships the same feature natively. If the answer is "nothing", it's a 1.

## 6. Leverage type (w10)

*Code and media scale without you. Labour doesn't.*

- **5** — Software or media: sells and delivers while she sleeps, marginal cost near zero, one unit of work serves every customer.
- **4** — A product with a thin service wrapper — mostly automated, occasional human touch.
- **3** — Productised service: fixed scope, templates and tooling do most of the work, delivery time falls with each repetition.
- **2** — Custom service with reusable components. Revenue still tracks hours, but the hours get more valuable.
- **1** — Pure billable hours. Income is capped by the calendar, and the cap is low for someone not working full-time.
- **0** — Requires managing a labour force or physical operations to scale at all.

Be honest about "software" ideas that are really a service with a dashboard. If the founder
is in the loop for every customer, it's a 2 or 3 regardless of how much code exists.

## 7. Capital required (w7)

*Cash needed to reach first revenue, given a self-funded solo founder.*

- **5** — Under £2k. Laptop, subscriptions, a domain.
- **4** — £2-10k. Some contracted design or development help, tooling, a little paid testing.
- **3** — £10-30k. Meaningful build or inventory before anyone pays.
- **2** — £30-100k. Real runway required before revenue.
- **1** — £100-250k, or outside capital needed before v1 exists.
- **0** — Above £250k, or the business is structurally impossible without institutional funding, licensing, or physical assets.

## 8. Time-to-first-revenue (w10)

*From starting to the first pound in the account — not to product-market fit.*

Weighted heavily because a part-time self-funded founder's real constraint is how long she
can sustain unpaid effort while the idea is still unproven.

- **5** — Under 30 days. She could sell it before building it.
- **4** — 1-3 months.
- **3** — 3-6 months.
- **2** — 6-9 months.
- **1** — 9-12 months, or the first revenue depends on a slow enterprise or public-sector cycle.
- **0** — Over 12 months, or gated behind regulatory approval, licensing, or a build that must be complete before anyone can pay.

Audience-first and media businesses almost always land at 1 or 2 from a standing start,
however fast the writing is. Say so rather than assuming momentum.

## 9. Secular tailwind (w5)

*Is the world moving toward this over five to ten years?*

- **5** — A strong structural driver with budget already attached and money already moving: AI adoption spend in professional services, energy-transition mandates, new regulatory reporting obligations.
- **4** — A clear multi-year trend with growing budgets, though not yet urgent for the buyer.
- **3** — Real but slow: the direction is right, no dedicated budget line exists yet.
- **2** — Flat market. Growth must be taken from competitors.
- **1** — Mild structural decline, or dependent on a trend that has already peaked.
- **0** — Actively shrinking, or the tailwind is the thing most likely to eliminate the product (particularly: work that a general-purpose model will absorb within two years).

Deliberately the lightest weight. Tailwinds are the easiest thing to feel confident about
and the least predictive of whether anyone pays next quarter.

## 10. Ceiling (w10)

*Does the honest best case clear £200k in one year?*

£200k of revenue net of direct delivery costs, within twelve months of starting — see
`founder-profile.md`. Derive it, never assert it: write out price × units × retention, and
say where the units come from. "Best case" means everything goes right, not everything goes
perfectly.

- **5** — £500k+ plausible in year one on a unit path she could actually service.
- **4** — £200-500k in year one.
- **3** — £100-200k in year one; clears £200k in year two.
- **2** — £50-100k. A real income, not the target.
- **1** — £20-50k. A good side project.
- **0** — Under £20k, or structurally capped: too few possible buyers, price too low to reach the number at any realistic volume, or delivery capacity binds before revenue does.

The sanity check that catches most inflated ceilings: at the stated price, how many
customers are needed, and can one person serve that many while still selling? If serving
them requires hiring, the direct-cost deduction has to come out of the number.

## Worked example

**Idea:** A monthly subscription giving buy-side generalist analysts AI-assisted deep-dive
primers on unfamiliar sectors — the thing she used to spend three days building before an
initiation. £150/month, sold to individual analysts and small teams.

| Criterion | W | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distribution | 15 | 4/5 | The buyer *is* her network. Perhaps 30 named analysts and PMs she can message directly, extended by the husband's sell-side and hedge fund contacts. Not 50+ with a repeatable engine, so 4 rather than 5. |
| Demand intensity | 15 | 2/5 | The pain is real and she has personally felt it, but no one has offered money and nobody's currently paying for a close substitute. No evidence caps this at 2, and it is the cheapest thing to fix. |
| Founder-market fit | 12 | 5/5 | Eight years doing exactly this job, plus the AI-adoption remit. She is the buyer, speaks the vocabulary, and can produce the artifact at credible quality on day one. |
| Revenue structure | 8 | 4/5 | Monthly subscription. Not contracted or embedded, so churn is a live risk once curiosity fades. |
| Defensibility | 8 | 2/5 | Judgement and taste in the output, but no compounding asset. A capable competitor — or a research platform adding a feature — matches it within months. |
| Leverage | 10 | 4/5 | One primer serves every subscriber. Human editorial time per issue keeps it off a 5. |
| Capital | 7 | 5/5 | Subscriptions, a domain, model API credits. Under £2k. |
| Time-to-first-revenue | 10 | 5/5 | She can sell three pilot subscriptions from her contacts before writing the second issue. |
| Tailwind | 5 | 4/5 | AI adoption spend in professional services is real and budgets exist. Marked down because the same wave lets buyers do this themselves. |
| Ceiling | 10 | 2/5 | £150/mo × 100 subscribers × ~10 months average retention ≈ £150k gross, and 100 individual subscribers is an optimistic year-one number from a 30-person warm base. Realistically £60-90k. Clearing £200k needs team licences at £1-2k/month, which is a different sale into a different buying process. |

**Total: 72.6/100 — but the ceiling gate fires (2/5, under £100k), so the verdict is
Marginal, not Strong.**

This is exactly what the gates are for. Exceptional founder-market fit, near-zero capital,
and revenue inside a month produce a number that looks like a green light, and the idea
still cannot reach the founder's stated bar. Averaging would have hidden that; the gate
surfaces it. And the gap between "72.6, capped" and a flat 45 is informative — this idea is
one structural change away from being good, rather than broadly weak.

The honest reshape is to sell team licences to funds rather than individual seats, which
lifts the ceiling to 4 and revenue structure to 5, at the cost of time-to-first-revenue
dropping to 3 and distribution to 3, since the buyer becomes a research director inside a
procurement process rather than a peer answering a message. That version scores about 73
uncapped and is the one worth testing.

Note that the report leads with the deduction, not the enthusiasm, and that the ceiling was
computed rather than claimed. That is the whole discipline.
