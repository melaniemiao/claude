# Replication watchlist

These are popular books that present themselves as science reporting. Some are good
books. None of them belong in a top-ten ranked by scientific robustness, and each entry
below says exactly why.

Two things this file is for:

1. **A filter.** Before finalizing a top ten, check the list against this file. If a
   candidate appears here, it either drops out or moves to a clearly caveated Tier C
   slot with the specific problem named.
2. **An honest answer.** When a user asks "is *[book]* legit?", give them the specific
   finding and what happened to it, not a verdict. "The core observation is real, this
   particular chapter isn't" is nearly always the true answer and it's more useful than
   either endorsement or dismissal.

**Tone matters here.** Most of these authors are not frauds — they are people who wrote
about a literature that later moved. Report what happened to the evidence, not on the
author's character. The exceptions where actual data fraud has been established are
marked, and even there, stick to what has been formally found.

## Science-branded books whose central claim weakened

**Why We Sleep** (Matthew Walker, 2017). Sleep is genuinely important and much of the
underlying physiology is real. But independent scrutiny documented a series of factual
errors and overstatements — misrepresented figures, claims attributed to studies that
don't support them, and mortality and disease risks stated far more strongly than the
literature warrants. Some corrections were subsequently made. Recommend the topic; don't
rank this book as rigorous.

**The Body Keeps the Score** (Bessel van der Kolk, 2014). That trauma has physiological
and not merely narrative effects is well supported, and the book gave many readers real
language for their experience — which is why it has sold so enormously. The weaker parts
are the specific therapeutic claims: the mechanistic account of EMDR, the evidence for
neurofeedback, and some of the neuroimaging interpretation all run ahead of the
literature. Tier C at best, and only with that said.

**Blink** (Malcolm Gladwell, 2005). Substantially built on social-priming research and
on implicit-association measures. Much of the priming work failed to replicate, and
meta-analyses of the Implicit Association Test found its ability to predict individual
behavior to be weak. Fun, influential, not evidence.

**Outliers** (Gladwell, 2008). The 10,000-hour rule. See the main library entry —
deliberate practice explains a modest and highly domain-dependent share of performance
variance, and the researcher whose work it was based on objected to the popularization.

**The Tipping Point** (Gladwell, 2000). The "law of the few" — that a handful of
connectors and mavens drive social epidemics — has held up poorly in subsequent network
research on diffusion.

**Grit** (Angela Duckworth, 2016). Meta-analytic work found that grit correlates so
strongly with the established personality trait of conscientiousness that it adds
little, and that it predicts performance and retention much more weakly than the book
suggests. Duckworth has engaged with these critiques publicly, which is to their credit.

**Mindset** (Carol Dweck, 2006). The growth-mindset idea is real but far smaller than
the book implies. Meta-analyses found average effects on achievement close to zero, and
large preregistered trials found small effects concentrated among lower-achieving
students. A modest, targeted intervention was popularized as a general theory of
success.

**Presence** (Amy Cuddy, 2015) and the power-posing literature. The hormonal claim —
that posture changes testosterone and cortisol — failed to replicate, and one of the
original study's co-authors publicly stated they no longer believed the effect was real.
Some later work supports a small self-reported feelings-of-power effect; the physiology
claim is the one that collapsed.

**Willpower** (Roy Baumeister & John Tierney, 2011) and ego depletion generally. The
central claim that self-control is a depletable resource drawing on glucose failed a
large multi-lab preregistered replication. The topic is live; this account of it is not
supported.

**Emotional Intelligence** (Daniel Goleman, 1995). Hugely influential in management. The
headline claim — that EQ matters far more than IQ for life outcomes, often quoted as an
80/20 split — was not supported by the research it gestured at. Measured emotional
intelligence adds modest predictive value over established personality and cognitive
measures.

**Predictably Irrational** (Dan Ariely, 2008) and **The Honest Truth About Dishonesty**
(2012). Several studies associated with this work have been subject to formal
data-fraud findings and retraction, including the widely cited insurance-form
honesty-pledge study. This is a stronger category of problem than replication failure
and should be stated plainly if the books come up.

**Freakonomics** (Levitt & Dubner, 2005). The signature abortion-and-crime analysis was
challenged over a coding error and specification choices, and the finding is not
settled. Other chapters vary in strength. Entertaining, not a robustness benchmark.

**The Power of Habit** (Charles Duhigg, 2012). Journalism built around the cue–routine–
reward loop. The loop is a reasonable framing device rather than a well-validated
mechanism, and several anchor anecdotes are told more tidily than the record supports.

**Influence** (Robert Cialdini, 1984). Genuinely closer to the line than most of this
list — reciprocity, scarcity, social proof, and commitment have real support. But some
of the specific studies cited across editions come from the shakiest corner of social
psychology, so treat it as Tier C: strong principles, uneven citations.

**Guns, Germs, and Steel** (Jared Diamond, 1997). Not a replication problem but a
disciplinary one: the geographic-determinist thesis is contested by many historians and
anthropologists as underdetermined by the evidence and inattentive to contingency and
agency. A serious argument, not a settled finding.

**12 Rules for Life** (Jordan Peterson, 2018). Mostly philosophy, mythology, and
clinical advice, which is fine — but where it makes empirical claims, some are weak, the
lobster-serotonin argument for human hierarchy being the most criticized. If it comes
up, place it in Part 2 as philosophy rather than in the ranked ten.

**The Anxious Generation** (Jonathan Haidt, 2024). Addresses a real and important
question, and the correlational evidence on adolescent mental health is worth taking
seriously. But the causal claim is actively contested by researchers who read the same
literature and find much smaller effects, and the book is too recent to have met the
"stood the test of time" bar regardless.

**Recent books generally.** Anything published in the last three or four years fails
criterion 4 by construction, however good it looks. It can be mentioned as promising;
it can't be ranked as durable.

## A different category: books that are not evidence-shaped at all

*The Secret*, *Rich Dad Poor Dad*, *The Power of Now*, and similar. These aren't
overstated science — they make no serious empirical claim, and in the case of
manifestation and law-of-attraction material the claims are affirmatively contradicted
by evidence. They can appear in Part 2 if the user's interests point that way, but
describe them accurately, and don't dress a claim that fails as merely unproven.
