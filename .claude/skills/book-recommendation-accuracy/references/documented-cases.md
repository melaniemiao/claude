# Documented cases

Popular books whose accuracy record is established, grouped by **failure type** rather
than by topic — because the type of failure is what determines the verdict. Plus
positive exemplars, which matter just as much: you can't rank books by accuracy without
a clear picture of what the top of the scale looks like.

This file is a starting point, not a complete register. Verify current status where you
can, and where a case isn't listed here, work it out with `verification-playbook.md`
rather than assuming a clean record.

**A note on tone.** Report what was found, not what it says about the author's soul.
Fabrication is a serious finding and should be stated plainly — but most entries here
are ordinary error, and describing those in the language of scandal is its own
inaccuracy.

## 1. Established fabrication or falsification

Tier D. These are not ranked as recommendations.

**A Million Little Pieces** (James Frey, 2003). Sold as memoir. An investigative report
in 2006 documented that central events were invented or substantially exaggerated,
including the length of the author's incarceration. The publisher subsequently added a
disclosure note and offered refunds to purchasers.

**Three Cups of Tea** (Greg Mortenson & David Oliver Relin, 2006). A television
investigation and a book-length rebuttal by Jon Krakauer in 2011 documented that key
narrative events — including the founding story — did not happen as described. Separate
findings concerned the associated charity's finances, which the Montana attorney general
settled.

**Imagine: How Creativity Works** (Jonah Lehrer, 2012). The author admitted fabricating
quotations attributed to Bob Dylan. The publisher withdrew the book from sale — an
unusually decisive action and a useful benchmark for what publishers do when a finding
is unambiguous.

**Roots** (Alex Haley, 1976). Presented as traced family history. Genealogical
researchers were unable to substantiate the central lineage claims, and Haley settled a
plagiarism suit brought by another author over passages from a prior novel. Enormously
important as a cultural document; not reliable as genealogy or history.

**The Education of Little Tree** (Forrest Carter, 1976). Marketed as an authentic
Cherokee childhood memoir. The author was later identified as a white segregationist
writer using a pseudonym, and the memoir was fiction. The archetype of falsified
authorship.

**Go Ask Alice** (published anonymously, 1971). Presented for decades as a real teenager's
diary. It was written by an adult author, and the diary framing was invention.

**1421: The Year China Discovered America** (Gavin Menzies, 2002). A bestseller whose
central claims historians and specialists in Chinese maritime history rejected
comprehensively, finding the documentary basis unsupported. The archetype of a popular
history with no scholarly support at all.

## 2. Composite characters and undisclosed reconstruction

Tier C to D depending entirely on **disclosure**. The practice is legitimate; concealing
it is not.

**In Cold Blood** (Truman Capote, 1966). Foundational to the true-crime genre and
presented as wholly factual. Subsequent research into investigative case files indicates
that the closing graveyard scene did not occur and that other passages were shaped for
effect. Read it as the origin of narrative nonfiction, not as a record.

**Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil** (John Berendt, 1994). The author has
acknowledged rearranging the chronology and constructing some scenes, including material
involving events they did not witness. Disclosed after the fact rather than in the book.

**Bringing Down the House** (Ben Mezrich, 2002) and the author's later books.
Acknowledged composite characters and recreated dialogue in books presented as accounts
of real events. Judge these against what the front matter actually tells the reader.

**Fire and Fury** (Michael Wolff, 2018). The author's own note concedes uncertainty
about some accounts, and multiple named subjects disputed quotations attributed to them.
Treat specific quotes as contested, whatever one makes of the broader picture.

## 3. Misrepresented or misread sources

Tier B to C. The book cites something real and says something the source doesn't
support. The most common failure in popular non-fiction, and the hardest to spot.

**Outrages** (Naomi Wolf, 2019). The central argument rested on a misreading of the legal
term "death recorded," which indicated a sentence was *not* carried out. The error was
identified by a historian during a live radio interview, and the US edition was
subsequently pulled by its publisher. The clearest available illustration of a single
terminological error collapsing a thesis.

**Why We Sleep** (Matthew Walker, 2017). Independent scrutiny documented misrepresented
figures and claims attributed to studies that do not support them, alongside risk
estimates stated more strongly than the literature warrants. Some corrections followed.
The topic is real; the sourcing is unreliable.

**The Hot Zone** (Richard Preston, 1994). Widely credited with dramatizing filovirus
symptoms well beyond the clinical reality — the vivid tissue-liquefaction imagery in
particular is not what the disease does. Reported as fact, written as thriller.

**Freakonomics** (Levitt & Dubner, 2005). The signature abortion-and-crime analysis was
challenged over a coding error and specification choices. An analytical error rather
than a reporting error, but the effect on the reader is the same.

## 4. Rejected by the relevant field

Where the discipline that owns the subject does not accept the account.

**Chariots of the Gods** (Erich von Däniken, 1968) and the ancient-astronaut genre.
Rejected in full by archaeologists. Listed because it remains in print and in wide
circulation.

**Holy Blood, Holy Grail** (1982) and the pseudohistorical bloodline literature
downstream of it, including the factual claims in the front matter of **The Da Vinci
Code** (2003) — the novel's assertion that its descriptions of artwork, architecture,
and documents are accurate does not hold.

**The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich** (William Shirer, 1960). A different and milder
case worth handling carefully. Shirer was a working journalist in Berlin, and the
reportage is valuable; historians have long criticized the interpretive framework and
the reliance on certain captured documents. Much of the objection is to the thesis
rather than to the facts — see section 6.

## 5. Accurate at publication, since overtaken

**Not errors.** The distinction matters and readers routinely miss it.

A book that reported the state of knowledge correctly and has since been superseded made
no mistake. Say "accurate at publication, now dated on X." Common examples: pre-genomic
biology books stating gene counts later revised; Cold War-era histories written before
Soviet archives opened; medical books predating a major trial; **Silent Spring** (1962),
whose ecological core was vindicated while specific human-health attributions outran what
was then knowable; **Cadillac Desert** (1986), whose reporting held while the policy
landscape moved on.

The test: could the author have known better at the time, with the sources then
available? If no, it isn't an accuracy problem.

## 6. Contested thesis, sound facts

Also not accuracy problems — and this is the distinction the ranking most often gets
wrong. Rank the facts, then note the interpretive dispute separately.

**Guns, Germs, and Steel** (Jared Diamond, 1997) — the geographic-determinist thesis is
disputed by many historians and anthropologists; the factual furniture largely holds.

**Sapiens** (Yuval Noah Harari, 2011/2014) — the recurring criticism is confidence
outrunning evidence on contested reconstructions rather than misstatement of established
fact. Closer to the line than Diamond, since stating a contested reconstruction flatly is
itself a kind of inaccuracy, but the failure is one of certainty, not invention.

**The Better Angels of Our Nature** (Steven Pinker, 2011) — the violence-decline thesis is
contested on statistical grounds by serious critics; the underlying datasets are cited
and checkable.

When you place a book here, say plainly: *the facts hold, the argument is disputed.*
Readers are well served by that sentence and rarely get it.

## 7. Exemplary correction behavior

Worth surfacing, because correction behavior is the best available predictor of the
errors nobody has found yet.

**Into Thin Air** (Jon Krakauer, 1997). Disputed by other participants in the 1996 Everest
disaster. Krakauer engaged the disputes directly and added a substantial postscript to
later editions addressing them. The model response: neither capitulation nor silence.

**Nudge: The Final Edition** (Thaler & Sunstein, 2021). Substantially rewritten, with
material the authors no longer stood behind removed rather than quietly retained.

**The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks** (Rebecca Skloot, 2010). Uses reconstructed
dialogue — and says so, explaining the method and sourcing in a note. This is how
disclosure is supposed to work, and why the book belongs here rather than in section 2.

## 8. Positive exemplars — what Tier A looks like

Use these to calibrate the top of the scale.

**Bad Blood** (John Carreyrou, 2018). Reporting conducted under sustained legal threat
from the subject's counsel, published with named sources, and subsequently borne out in
a federal criminal trial. About as thoroughly stress-tested as a book gets.

**The Making of the Atomic Bomb** (Richard Rhodes, 1986). Exhaustively sourced, and
regarded as accurate by physicists and historians of science who lived through or study
the events described.

**Say Nothing** (Patrick Radden Keefe, 2019) and **Empire of Pain** (2021). Densely
documented, built substantially on archival and oral-history material, and written under
the same kind of adversarial pressure that tests sourcing.

**The Warmth of Other Suns** (Isabel Wilkerson, 2010). Built on more than a thousand
interviews conducted over roughly a decade, with the selection method disclosed.

**SPQR** (Mary Beard, 2015). A Cambridge classicist who states repeatedly and explicitly
what the evidence cannot establish. The habit of marking the boundary of the knowable is
the strongest single indicator of a reliable author.

**The Emperor of All Maladies** (Siddhartha Mukherjee, 2010). A practitioner writing
about their own field, careful throughout about which treatments earned their reputation
in trials and which were enthusiasm.

**Killers of the Flower Moon** (David Grann, 2017). Archival investigative work that
surfaced primary material others had missed, with the documentary basis laid out.
