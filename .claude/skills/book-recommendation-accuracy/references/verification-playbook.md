# Verification playbook

How to establish what a book's accuracy record actually is, rather than guessing from
its reputation. Read this whenever you're assessing a book whose record you don't
already know.

## Where the record lives

Accuracy problems surface in predictable places. In rough order of value:

1. **Reviews in the field's own publications.** A historian reviewing a history book in
   a history journal checks things a newspaper reviewer never will. This is the highest-
   yield source and the most commonly skipped.
2. **The author's own corrections.** Errata pages, revised-edition prefaces, postscripts
   answering critics. Search the author's name with "correction" or "responds to".
3. **Adversarial scrutiny.** Litigation, a subject who disputes their portrayal, a rival
   author's rebuttal, an investigative piece. Hostile parties are thorough.
4. **Dedicated critiques.** Some books attract a full-length rebuttal — an essay, a
   monograph, a long review. If one exists, it is the densest source available.
5. **Publisher actions.** Withdrawal from sale, a cancelled edition, an added note,
   refunds offered. These are strong signals because publishers act reluctantly.

Useful searches: the title with "errors", "fact check", "inaccuracies", "debunked",
"retracted"; the author with "corrected" or "responded"; and the title with the name of
the relevant discipline ("historians", "epidemiologists", "archaeologists").

Encyclopedia "Reception" and "Controversy" sections are a **lead generator, not a
source** — use them to find the underlying review or investigation, then rely on that.

## Which challenges count

Not all criticism is evidence of inaccuracy. Weigh it by what the critic actually did:

- **Counts heavily:** a specialist naming a specific claim, the source it rests on, and
  what that source actually says. One of these outweighs any number of admiring reviews.
- **Counts:** a subject of the book disputing a scene they were present for — though
  they have their own interests, so report it as a dispute, not a finding.
- **Counts little:** a reviewer who dislikes the thesis, finds the tone objectionable,
  or objects to the author's politics without identifying a factual error.
- **Counts as a positive:** a book attacked hard by motivated opponents that survived
  intact. Surviving scrutiny is better evidence of accuracy than never attracting any.

When a book has been challenged and the author has answered, report both and say where
it landed. If it didn't land anywhere, say that — an open dispute is a real state of
affairs and pretending to adjudicate it is its own inaccuracy.

## The absence-of-errors trap

No documented errors can mean two opposite things: the book is accurate, or nobody
checked. Before treating a clean record as evidence, ask whether this book was ever
subjected to real scrutiny — was it prominent enough, did it make claims anyone had a
stake in disputing, is there a discipline that would have noticed?

An obscure book with no documented errors and a famous, contested book with a handful of
documented errors are not comparable, and the second is usually the safer recommendation.
Say which situation you're in rather than letting silence read as a clean bill of health.

## Judge against the genre's disclosed conventions

Different genres have different legitimate practices, and applying journalism's
standards to memoir produces nonsense verdicts. What matters is whether the book
**discloses** its method.

- **Narrative nonfiction** routinely reconstructs dialogue from interviews and
  documents. Disclosed in a source note, this is standard practice, not fabrication.
  Undisclosed — presented as though transcribed — it is a serious problem.
- **Memoir** is a single perspective recalled years later. Judge it on whether
  checkable external facts hold and whether the author acknowledges the limits of
  memory, not on whether the dialogue is verbatim.
- **Investigative journalism** should name sources or explain why it can't, and its
  central claims should be independently corroborated.
- **Academic trade books** should have claim-level citations. When they don't, that is
  itself the finding.

The disqualifying move in every genre is the same: presenting invented or uncertain
material as established, without telling the reader.

## Red flags and green flags

**Red flags** — predict inaccuracy before you've checked anything:

- A bibliography but no claim-level notes, so no assertion can be traced.
- Quotations with no attributed source, especially from historical or famous figures.
- "Studies show" or "researchers found" without naming the study.
- Suspiciously well-formed anecdotes where every detail serves the argument.
- A thesis resting on a single unnamed source, or on the author as sole witness.
- Round, memorable numbers presented with more precision than any method could yield.
- The author is the hero of their own reporting.
- No acknowledgment anywhere that something is unknown or uncertain.

**Green flags** — correlate with accuracy:

- Endnotes tied to specific pages and claims.
- Explicit statements of what is unknown, disputed, or inferred.
- Named sources for contested claims, with the disagreement reported rather than resolved.
- A published erratum, a corrected edition, or a postscript engaging critics.
- Speculation flagged as speculation in the text, not buried in a note.

## When you cannot resolve a dispute

Say so, specifically. "Two historians have challenged the casualty figures; the author
defended them in the 2011 edition; I could not establish who is right" is a genuinely
useful sentence. Manufacturing a verdict — or a citation to support one — is exactly the
failure this skill exists to catch, and doing it here is worse than doing it anywhere
else.

## Checking your own output

Before delivering, reread every specific claim you made about a book or author and ask
whether you actually know it or merely inferred it from context. The highest-risk items,
in order:

1. **Author credentials and institutions.** Never derive these from the book's subject.
   Someone who wrote about neuroscience is not therefore a neuroscientist.
2. **Publication years and editions**, especially when your verdict is edition-specific.
3. **Attributed criticism** — who found the error. If you can't name the critic
   confidently, describe the finding without inventing a source for it.
4. **Numbers.** Sales figures, interview counts, page counts, casualty figures.

Where verification tools are available, check rather than recall. Where they aren't,
prefer the vaguer true statement to the precise guess.
