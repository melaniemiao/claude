Ten Ideas Worth Testing

One hundred business ideas, generated for founder-market fit and scored against the ten-criterion rubric. These are the ten that survived. They are not the ten most exciting — they are the ten where an unfair advantage, a reachable buyer, and arithmetic that reaches £200k happen to coincide. Seven of them are the same business model wearing different clothes, which is itself the finding.

How to read the scores

Each idea is scored 0–5 on ten criteria, weighted to 100. Distribution and demand carry the most weight (15 each) because they are the two things a founder cannot outwork. Tailwind carries the least (5) because it is the easiest thing to feel confident about and the least predictive at a one-year horizon.

Three criteria are gates: distribution or demand at 1 or below, or a ceiling at 2 or below, caps the verdict at Marginal no matter how high the total. A fatal flaw should not be averaged away. Fifty-nine of the hundred ideas hit a gate; none of these ten did.

Why nothing scores above 5 on demand

Demand is scored on evidence, not on how good the argument is. Nobody has been asked for money on any of these, so every one is capped at 2–3. That single criterion is costing each idea 6–9 points right now, and two weeks of conversations would move the whole table further than any more brainstorming.

Why the mean is high

The hundred averaged 64.4, at the top of the rubric's normal band. That is not drift — the brief was to generate ideas selected for founder-market fit, and it worked: fit averaged 4.39 out of 5. Against a random idea set the same rubric runs considerably lower.

What "best case" means

Best case is everything going right, not everything going perfectly, measured as revenue net of direct delivery costs within twelve months of starting. It is derived from price × units × retention on every idea, never asserted.

Why these ten

Across the full hundred, the binding constraint was almost never capability or access. Founder-market fit averaged 4.39 and distribution 3.46 — both strong. What failed was leverage (the most frequent binding constraint, 35 times) and ceiling (59 ideas scored 2 or below). Those are the same problem twice: expertise sells instantly by the hour and caps at the calendar, while anything that scales starts from no audience and pays nothing for a year.

These ten escape that trap, and seven escape it the same way: institutional subscription research at £1,000–£2,500 a month, needing only nine to seventeen clients. That structure is the only one in the set where one unit of work serves many buyers and the price is high enough that a fifty-person warm network is large enough to fill it. The three exceptions — a fixed-fee diagnostic, an annual benchmark, and a fractional role — earn their place differently, and each entry says how.

Six of the ten sit at the built environment or energy, rather than in pure finance. That is not an accident of generation: the rubric rewards ideas that need two of the founder's domains at once, because that is where defensibility comes from.

Where to start

Run one test, not ten. Every idea here is capped by the same missing thing — evidence that someone will pay. The highest-value fortnight available is the retrofit test: ask three real-estate fund contacts what their MEES capex exposure is and whether anyone has modelled it. It costs nothing, and it moves the only idea in a hundred that scored Exceptional.

Then note what the shortlist has in common. If institutional subscription research is the shape that works, the choice between these ideas is mostly a choice of subject — and the subject can follow whichever conversation goes best. Testing three subjects with the same offer is cheaper than committing to one.

Two things worth arguing with. The prices are assumptions: if £1,200 a month is wrong for a fund's research budget, most of these ceilings move with it. And the tailwind scores penalise anything relying on independent research being paid for in cash, because the FCA's 2024–25 re-bundling rules route research spend back through brokers who execute. If that reading is wrong, several of these scores rise.
